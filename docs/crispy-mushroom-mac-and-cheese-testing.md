# Crispy Mushroom Mac and Cheese — Kitchen Test Plan

**Recipe:** [Crispy Mushroom Mac and Cheese (Weeknight)](../recipes/crispy-mushroom-mac-and-cheese.yaml)  
**Status:** Proposed adaptation. **Not yet kitchen-tested.** Nothing in the observation tables below is filled in, and no result should be treated as verified until someone cooks it and records what happened.

This document is a place to record real outcomes, not a claim that the recipe already works. It also separates principles that are well established from predictions that still need testing in this specific dish.

---

## 1. What this recipe is trying to achieve

| Goal | What to look for |
|---|---|
| Deep mushroom flavour | Browning on the mushrooms before they meet the sauce; no watery, steamed flavour |
| Crispy top | Golden, crisp bread and browned cheese; bread not soggy |
| Browned bottom | Dark golden patches where the pasta met the pan; portions lift with some crust attached |
| Creamy interior | Sauce coats the pasta; no dry or greasy centre |
| Distinct mushroom pieces | Visible, substantial slices rather than a purée |
| Savoury depth | Browned mushrooms, onion, garlic, cheddar, and optional soy sauce |
| Special-occasion richness | Enough fat and cheese to feel indulgent without requiring specialty cheeses |

A crispy bottom is an **experimental outcome**, not a guarantee. The method below is conservative; it is written to make browning more likely without asking anyone to preheat a pan to smoking or leave a cheese-coated pan unattended over direct heat.

## 2. Equipment

### Preferred

- **12-inch flat cast-iron skillet** (about 30 cm). Gives a wide, continuous contact surface and enough room for a 2.5–3 cm layer.
- Oven mitts, a thin metal spatula, a baking sheet for toasting the bread, and a saucepan for the sauce.

### Alternative

- **Shallow 9 × 13-inch metal baking pan.** Similar surface area. Many metal baking pans are not intended for stovetop use, so skip the stovetop pre-sear and extend the oven bake by about 5 minutes.

### Experimental

- **Ridged cast-iron grill pan.** Legitimate to test, but not assumed to be better. The mixture flows into the channels, which increases contact area in some places, while the ridges lift the mixture away from the pan in others. It can also make portions harder to remove and the pan harder to clean.

### Safety and care

- Remove any **silicone handle cover** before putting the skillet in the oven.
- Do **not** use a **glass lid** during high-temperature baking unless its individual temperature rating explicitly allows it.
- Do not pour cold liquid into a hot cast-iron pan, and do not put a hot pan into cold water.
- Cast iron retains heat. Set the hot pan on a trivet or a dry folded towel, not directly on a counter.
- If the pan is not explicitly stovetop-safe, do not use direct heat for the bottom crust.
- If using the broiler, confirm the pan is broiler-safe and stay at the oven.

### Comparing flat and ridged cast iron

Record the same mixture, oven, and timings for each pan, then compare:

1. **Total crust formation** — how much of the base is browned, not just the rim.
2. **Browning consistency** — patches, stripes, or an even sheet of crust.
3. **Ease of removing intact portions** — does a portion hold together on the spatula?
4. **Sticking** — how much crust stays in the pan.
5. **Cleaning effort** — time and scrubbing required after a short soak.

Do not assume the ridged pan wins on total crust. The channels add contact area, but the peaks reduce it and can make release worse.

## 3. Proposed method (primary, conservative)

1. Brown the mushrooms in batches over medium-high heat, with enough room for moisture to escape. Let the water cook off before expecting browning.
2. Cook the onion in the same pan, add garlic late, return the mushrooms, and add optional soy sauce.
3. Make a butter-flour roux, add milk, simmer to a coating consistency, then take it **off the heat** before adding Dijon and cheddar.
4. Par-cook the pasta about 2 minutes short of al dente and reserve pasta water.
5. Combine pasta, mushrooms, and sauce; spread in the lightly oiled skillet in a shallow, even layer.
6. **Optional stovetop pre-sear:** 3–4 minutes over medium heat, rotating once, checking the edge with a thin spatula. Never unattended.
7. Bake uncovered at 210°C (410°F) for 15 minutes. Add pre-toasted garlic bread and bake 8–10 minutes more. Optional broiler 2–3 minutes only if the pan is broiler-safe.
8. Rest 5 minutes, then lift portions with a thin spatula.

### Why this is conservative

- No extreme preheating and no smoking-hot pan.
- Direct heat is moderate and time-bounded, with a check step.
- The pan is lightly greased, not flooded with fat.
- The oven finish is uncovered so steam can escape.
- The rest period gives the base a chance to set before lifting.

### Known risks

| Risk | Mitigation |
|---|---|
| Sticking, especially with cheese at the base | Light grease; moderate heat; check after 2–3 minutes; do not force the spatula |
| Burning the base during the stovetop phase | Stay at the pan; use medium heat; stop at light browning, not blackening |
| Soggy top | Pre-toast the bread; add it only for the last 8–10 minutes |
| Grainy or greasy sauce | Add cheese off the heat; avoid boiling after cheese; keep the sauce loose |
| Dry centre | Do not overbake; pull when the sauce bubbles at the edges and the centre is still creamy |
| Pan damage from thermal shock | No cold liquid in a hot pan; no cold-water bath |
| Broiler flare or burnt bread | Watch constantly; 2–3 minutes maximum; keep the rack lower if needed |

## 4. Established principles vs. untested predictions

### Well established

- Mushrooms release substantial water; browning develops after that water evaporates, when the surface can exceed boiling.
- Maillard browning is a family of reactions between amino acids and reducing sugars; it is not one precise temperature threshold. It generally becomes noticeable on a dry surface in the 140–165°C range, depending on the food.
- Cheese sauces can turn grainy when the protein/fat/water emulsion breaks under excessive heat; adding cheese off the heat is a standard preventive technique.
- Pasta absorbs water during baking; par-cooking leaves room for that absorption.
- Toasted bread stays crisper than raw bread added to a wet surface.
- A shallow layer exposes more surface area and reduces insulation compared with a deep layer.

### Untested predictions for this recipe

- That a 3–4 minute stovetop pre-sear reliably creates a browned base without sticking in a 12-inch cast-iron skillet.
- That the ridged grill pan produces more useful crust than a flat pan.
- That a thin Parmesan layer at the base improves release or browning rather than sticking.
- That a fine breadcrumb layer under the pasta stays crisp in contact with the sauce (it probably will not; test it, do not assume it).
- That the exact 45 g flour / 600 ml milk / 250 g cheddar ratio gives the ideal creaminess after baking.
- That 5 minutes of resting is enough for clean portioning in every pan.

Label any claim from a single cook as an observation, not a result. Repeat at least twice before treating it as reliable.

## 5. Suggested cooking tests

Run these as separate attempts or split the batch if your pan and oven allow. Change one variable at a time where practical.

### Test A — Baseline

Use the recipe exactly as written, including the optional stovetop pre-sear if the pan is stovetop-safe. Record everything in the table below.

### Test B — Stovetop time

Compare **no stovetop pre-sear**, **3 minutes**, and **5 minutes** at medium heat. Keep the oven time the same. Look for the point where browning improves without sticking or bitterness.

### Test C — Flat vs. ridged cast iron

Use the same batch size and oven temperature. Compare crust coverage, consistency, release, sticking, and cleaning. Photograph the empty pan after serving, if convenient, to compare what stayed behind.

### Test D — Parmesan contact layer

Before adding the pasta, scatter **10 g finely grated Parmesan** in a thin layer over the greased base. Compare release and browning with the baseline. This is a test variation, not part of the primary method. It may stick or burn; do not use it on the first attempt.

### Test E — Fine crumb layer

Scatter **15 g panko or fine dry breadcrumbs** over the greased base before adding the pasta. Evaluate whether it stays crisp or turns into a soggy layer. Do not assume it will help.

### Test F — Bread timing

Compare adding the toasted garlic bread at the **start of baking**, at **8 minutes**, and at **12 minutes**. Record crispness and whether the bread absorbs sauce.

### Test G — Sauce looseness

Compare a sauce that just coats a spoon with one loosened by 2 tbsp pasta water. Record whether the baked centre is creamy, dry, or runny.

### Test H — Scale

Cook at **2 servings** and **8 servings** in appropriately sized pans. Do not simply bake a deep 8-serving batch in the 12-inch skillet; pan depth and surface area change the result. Note any changes to browning and time.

### Test I — Bread alternatives

Compare the default garlic bread with panko and with leftover sourdough. Record flavour, salt, cost, and crispness.

## 6. What to observe while cooking

- How much water the mushrooms release and how long it takes to evaporate.
- Whether the mushrooms brown in patches or evenly; whether the pan is crowded.
- Whether the onion browns or steams; whether the garlic catches.
- The sauce's consistency before baking and after adding cheese.
- Whether the sauce looks glossy or grainy.
- How much pasta water is used.
- How the mixture sits in the pan: depth, coverage, and any dry edges.
- Whether the stovetop phase sizzles, smells toasted, or smells acrid.
- How much the top browns before and after the bread goes on.
- Whether the centre is still creamy when the edges bubble.
- How cleanly portions release; how much crust remains in the pan.
- How the dish tastes after resting, and whether the mushroom flavour is pronounced.

### What the adaptation removes

- Multi-day homemade flatbread and its preferment.
- Separate garlic confit and its oil.
- Truffle tapenade.
- A multi-cheese specialty mixture.
- A separate crouton recipe.
- A separate long-caramelized onion recipe.

### What replaces it

- Ordinary dry macaroni or shells.
- A single mature-cheddar béchamel.
- Onion browned alongside the mushrooms.
- Purchased garlic bread, toasted.
- Well-browned cremini mushrooms, with optional porcini or soy sauce.

Record which substitutes you actually used. If you used the optional porcini, add its cost and note that the base recipe does not require it.

### Why each change was made

| Original component | Weeknight choice | Reason | Trade-off |
|---|---|---|---|
| Multi-day flatbread and croutons | Purchased garlic bread, toasted | Removes a long preferment and a second baking project | Less complex bread flavour and less control over salt |
| Garlic confit purée | Fresh garlic browned with the onion | Avoids a separate confit and a jar of oil | Less sweet, mellow garlic depth |
| Truffle tapenade | Well-browned cremini, optional porcini or soy | Truffle products are expensive and not a supermarket staple | Less truffle aroma; more mushroom-forward |
| Several specialty cheeses | One mature cheddar plus Parmesan | One cheese can carry the sauce when it is aged and the sauce is balanced | Less layered flavour and less melt complexity |
| Separate caramelized onions | Onion browned alongside the mushrooms | Saves a pan and 30–45 minutes | Less deep sweetness; more savoury than sweet |
| Specialty pasta | Ordinary macaroni or shells | Widely available and cheap | Less chewy, nutty pasta character |
| Elaborate cheese sauce | Cheddar béchamel with Dijon | Predictable, cheap, and less likely to break | Less tang and less specialized cheese flavour |
| Separate crouton recipe | Pre-toasted purchased bread | Fewer steps and less washing-up | Brand-dependent salt and garlic level |

These are deliberate compromises, not claims that the result is identical.

### Expected differences from the inspiration

- **More direct:** cheddar and browned cremini dominate; there is no truffle or multi-cheese layering.
- **Less sweet:** the onion is browned with the mushrooms rather than slowly caramelized on its own.
- **Simpler bread:** purchased garlic bread gives crunch and salt but not the flavour of freshly baked flatbread.
- **Variable bottom:** the bottom crust depends on the pan, moisture, and stovetop step; it is not a restaurant-style guarantee.
- **Still rich:** the béchamel, cheddar, butter, and Parmesan are intended to keep the dish special-occasion rich without specialty ingredients.

## 10. Adjustment guide for later attempts

| Problem | Likely cause | Next-attempt adjustment |
|---|---|---|
| Bottom sticks badly | Insufficient fat, heat too high, or sauce too wet | Light grease; medium heat; check sooner; reduce pasta water |
| Bottom is pale | Pan not hot enough, too much moisture, or no contact | Dry the base more; try 4 minutes stovetop; use a flat pan |
| Bottom is burnt | Stovetop too hot or too long | Lower heat; 2–3 minutes; move to oven sooner |
| Top is soggy | Bread added too early or not toasted | Pre-toast longer; add at 8 minutes; use panko |
| Top is too dark | Broiler too close or too long | Lower rack; 2 minutes; watch constantly |
| Sauce is grainy | Cheese added too hot or sauce boiled | Add cheese off heat; keep sauce below a boil; grate finely |
| Centre is dry | Overbaked or sauce too tight | Pull earlier; loosen sauce with pasta water; cover loosely if needed |
| Sauce is runny | Under-thickened or too much pasta water | Simmer roux/milk longer; add cheese gradually; rest longer |
| Mushroom flavour is weak | Mushrooms steamed, crowded, or underbrowned | Brown in smaller batches; wait for water to evaporate; add soy |
| Dish is too salty | Cheddar, Parmesan, garlic bread, and soy all add salt | Salt the sauce late; use unsalted butter; reduce soy |
| Portions fall apart | Not rested or too much sauce | Rest 5 minutes; use a thin spatula; loosen only enough to flow |
